It was supposed to be just another normal day, but one strange morning changed everything I knew about the world.
Hi, I am Mark, and I live in an old radio observatory high up in the mountains, far away from the chaos of the city. I always thought being isolated was a good thing... until one morning, at exactly six fourteen in the morning, the sun never rose.
And that is when everything started to go wrong.
At six fourteen in the morning, the sun failed to rise. At six fifteen, the dead began calling home.
The first thing that disappeared was the birdsong.
I remember that because I had been awake all night, listening to the wind scrape against the observatory rusted antenna. The mountain was usually full of noise before sunrise... birds, insects, distant trucks grinding up the valley road. At six thirteen, all of it stopped.
I checked the clock above the radio desk.
Six fourteen.
The eastern horizon was supposed to turn orange in six minutes.
Instead, something black spilled over the mountains.
It didn't roll like fog. It climbed.
A thick, ash-colored darkness poured down the slopes and swallowed the pine trees one row at a time. It moved against the wind. It flowed around boulders, over rooftops, through the narrow streets of the city below.
Then every streetlight in the valley went out simultaneously.
I watched the city vanish behind the glass.
For one stupid moment, I thought it was a power cut.
Then the radio screamed.
Every frequency, every channel, every station collapsed into a single low tone that vibrated through the desk and into my teeth. The observatory's digital clocks blinked. My mobile phone went black. The diesel generator coughed once and stopped.
Outside, somewhere below the mountain, a woman began screaming.
She screamed for eleven seconds.
Then she said, in a perfectly calm voice:
"Yes. Of course."
And walked into the fog.
I didn't see her again.
I spent the next thirty minutes trying to call the city. No network. No emergency lines. No internet. I tried the observatory landline, which had been disconnected for years.
It rang.
I dropped it.
It rang again.
On the fourth ring, someone answered.
A little boy whispered, Why did you leave us outside?
Then the line went dead.
I was alone in a radio observatory built in nineteen sixty-eight, three kilometers above the nearest town. I had thirty days of emergency rations, two car batteries, a hand-cranked receiver, a telescope, a few flashlights, and a diesel generator with a questionable fuel reserve.
I had always considered myself fortunate to work somewhere remote.
By the end of the first day, I understood that remoteness was not the same thing as safety.
And by the end of the first week, I would understand that the fog wasn't trying to get inside.
It was trying to get me to open the door.
Day One... Don't Look Outside.
I covered every window.
Black tarps first. Then aluminum foil. Then blankets, stapled into the wooden frames until not a single strip of daylight could get through.
There was no daylight to block, of course.
That was the problem.
The observatory's windows faced east, and the room remained illuminated by a dim, colorless twilight that seemed to originate from nowhere. It wasn't light in the normal sense. It had no direction. No shadows. It simply made objects visible without revealing what was illuminating them.
I tested this with a chair.
I moved it beneath the ceiling lamp, then turned the lamp off.
The chair remained visible.
I moved it into the corridor and shut the door.
The chair remained visible in my memory, perfectly clear, as though I were still staring at it.
That was when I noticed my hands shaking.
I wrote down the first rule on the back of an old weather chart.
Rule One... Never look directly into the fog.
The second rule came at eleven thirty-two in the morning.
Three knocks sounded at the front door.
I froze.
The observatory was surrounded by a kilometer of exposed rock and a narrow service road. Nobody could reach the entrance without making a great deal of noise on the gravel.
Three more knocks.
Then a voice.
Hello?
A man's voice. Young. Tired.
"Please. I know somebody's in there."
I reached for the door handle.
Stopped.
The voice sounded familiar, but I couldn't place it. It had the intimate, almost embarrassing quality of someone speaking from a childhood memory.
Please, he said. It is cold.
I approached the security monitor beside the entrance. The camera feed was dead, showing a gray screen with a single white horizontal line.
The man outside began crying.
Then he said my name.
Not shouted.
Not called.
Whispered, as if his mouth were pressed against the wood.
I stepped backward.
He stopped crying.
You are not going to check? he asked.
I didn't answer.
A long silence followed.
Then the voice said, with mild disappointment, You used to be kinder.
The footsteps retreated down the steps.
I watched the clock until midnight.
At two seventeen in the morning, three knocks sounded from inside the locked supply room.
I had checked it twice.
It was empty.
I didn't sleep that night.
Day Two... The Street Below.
The fog had swallowed the mountain road.
I could see it through a tiny gap beneath the western tarp, where the fabric didn't quite meet the stone wall. The darkness outside was dense enough to look solid. It moved in slow vertical ripples, like curtains breathing in a room with no air.
At nine in the morning, I powered the ham radio.
The receiver was old but reliable, a heavy military-surplus unit connected to an external antenna. I worked through emergency frequencies, repeating my coordinates and asking for any station to respond.
Nothing.
I tried again.
Nothing.
Then a man's voice broke through the static.
...any station receiving, this is...
Static swallowed his words.
I adjusted the tuning dial.
...do not approach the eastern perimeter...
The voice was interrupted by a rapid sequence of clicks.
Then, faintly:
...if you can hear your own voice, disconnect immediately.
I stared at the radio.
The speaker crackled.
My voice came through.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
It was my voice, down to the slight rasp I got when I was tired.
I hadn't transmitted those words.
The recording continued.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
Then again.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
The same sentence, repeated every twelve seconds.
I switched the radio off.
The voice continued for three more repetitions.
Only then did the speaker fall silent.
I disconnected the power leads and wrapped the receiver in a blanket.
That evening, I discovered a wet footprint on the floor outside my bedroom.
Barefoot.
Pointing inward.
There was no matching footprint pointing away.
Day Three... The First Calculation.
I spent the day establishing what I could control.
Water: approximately twenty-six days if rationed carefully.
Food: thirty days, possibly longer if I ate once a day.
Batteries: two car batteries, both old. The generator's starter battery had been removed and connected to a makeshift lighting circuit. I could power the radio, a few lamps, and essential instruments, but not indefinitely.
Fuel: enough to run the generator intermittently, provided I didn't waste it.
I wrote everything on a chalkboard.
Numbers helped.
Numbers didn't whisper.
At eighteen forty, I heard footsteps circling the building.
Slow, measured steps on gravel.
They began at the front entrance, passed beneath the eastern windows, continued around the western wall, and returned to the door.
I listened to the entire circuit.
Four minutes and thirty seconds.
The footsteps started again.
Four minutes and thirty seconds.
Again.
I went to the radio room and checked the wall clock.
It read eighteen forty.
I checked my wristwatch.
Eighteen forty.
The footsteps circled again.
When I looked back at the clock, it still read eighteen forty.
I waited.
The footsteps completed another circuit.
Eighteen forty.
I pulled the clock off the wall and removed its batteries.
The second hand stopped.
Outside, the footsteps stopped too.
For a few seconds, there was absolute silence.
Then something walked up the observatory steps and stood directly outside the door.
It did not knock.
It simply waited.
I sat on the floor with my back against the desk until morning.
At six fourteen in the morning, the footsteps walked away.
The clock, still without batteries, began ticking again.
Day Four... The Rules Change.
I woke on the floor beside the radio desk with a stiff neck and a mouth that tasted like copper.
The clock read six fourteen.
I had begun to hate those four digits.
I checked the power system. The car batteries had lost nearly a third of their charge overnight, even though I had disconnected every circuit.
The multimeter was old, but I tested it twice.
The numbers were the same.
Something was drawing power from the batteries.
I removed the leads, carried both batteries into the shielded equipment cabinet, and shut the metal door. When I checked them an hour later, the voltage had dropped again.
I wrote a new rule.
Rule Two... Never leave an electrical device connected longer than necessary. The fog consumes power.
That afternoon, I tried to look through the fog without looking directly at it.
I used a small shaving mirror, holding it around the corner of the building's western window. The mirror showed the gravel path, the outer wall, and a section of the pine forest.
For a moment, nothing moved.
Then I saw a person standing between the trees.
A woman in a long coat.
Her face was turned toward the mirror.
I lowered it immediately.
When I raised it again, she was closer.
I lowered it.
Raised it.
Closer.
Each time I checked, she had crossed another stretch of ground without any visible movement.
Eventually, her face filled the mirror.
She was smiling.
Not a wide, threatening smile. It was the gentle expression of somebody trying not to frighten a child.
She lifted one hand and tapped the glass from the other side of the mirror.
I threw it across the room.
It shattered against the wall.
For several minutes, I sat among the broken pieces, trying to convince myself that the mirror had merely distorted the image.
Then I noticed something on the floor.
One of the shards reflected the room behind me.
In that reflection, the door to the equipment cabinet was open.
In the actual room, it was closed.
I turned around.
The cabinet was still closed.
When I looked back at the shard, the reflected door had closed too.
I swept up every piece of glass and buried them beneath the basement stairs.
I didn't use a mirror again.
Day Five... The Voice on Channel Nineteen.
The first intelligible transmission arrived at fourteen0seven.
I had reconnected the radio using a short length of shielded cable and the smallest possible power draw. I kept my hand on the cutoff switch.
Static.
A faint whistle.
Then a woman speaking in a steady, professional voice.
Emergency advisory. All survivors should remain indoors. Avoid unshielded windows. Do not respond to voices originating outside your shelter.
I nearly cried.
The woman repeated the message.
Then she gave coordinates for a military evacuation site in the valley.
I copied them down.
She repeated the coordinates twice.
Then she said, This transmission is automated. If you can hear this message, you are not alone.
A pause.
Repeat. You are not alone.
I grabbed the microphone.
Unknown station, this is the mountain observatory. I am alive. I have supplies for approximately twenty-five days. Please confirm the evacuation coordinates.
The transmission stopped.
I waited ten seconds.
Then twenty.
I was about to switch frequencies when the woman returned.
Mountain observatory.
My hand went cold.
Your supplies are insufficient.
I had not mentioned my location beyond my initial call. The coordinates were known, yes, but the phrase mountain observatory was too specific.
Please conserve your resources, she continued. Assistance is not guaranteed.
I leaned closer.
Who is this?
The static grew louder.
Then the woman said, You should not have answered the door on the first day.
The radio clicked off.
I had not answered the door.
I had almost opened it.
I had never told anyone about that.
I unplugged the receiver and left it on the desk.
That night, I dreamed of standing outside the observatory, looking at my own bedroom window.
Inside, somebody was sleeping in my bed.
I could see his face clearly.
It was mine.
Day Six... The Emergency Frequency.
I found the first piece of writing inside the old meteorological logbook.
It was tucked between pages dated forty-three years earlier, written in faded blue ink.
Six fourteen. No sunrise.
Personnel instructed to remain in observation chamber.
All external lighting to remain active.
The next entry was dated the following day.
Staff member H. disappeared from locked quarters. No evidence of forced entry.
Then:
Heard H. on the radio. H. is not transmitting from the valley.
The entries became increasingly erratic.
They imitate the dead.
Do not trust the clocks.
Do not sleep facing the dome.
The final entry had been pressed so hard into the paper that the pen had torn through several pages.
WE ARE NOT RECEIVING THEM.
THEY ARE RECEIVING US.
I spent hours looking for the author's name.
The logbook's cover listed the chief technician as Elias Voss.
I searched the observatory records. Voss had disappeared in November 1983 during a routine night shift. No body was recovered. The incident was classified as an accident involving the upper observation platform.
The observatory had been closed for two years afterward.
I read the report three times.
At the bottom was a handwritten note:
All optical instruments must be capped at six fourteen.
I went upstairs to the observation dome.
The telescope was pointed toward the eastern valley. Its lens cap lay on the floor beside the eyepiece.
I distinctly remembered putting it on the previous evening.
I picked it up.
There was a greasy black fingerprint on the inside of the cap.
It was still wet.
Day Seven... They Navigate by Shadow.
At three twenty-six in the morning, the radio switched itself on.
I woke to the sound of a woman whispering numbers.
...four hundred and eighty.
Static.
...four hundred and ninety.
A pause.
...five hundred.
Then the voice changed.
It became a man's voice, deep and distorted, speaking so slowly that each word seemed to take effort.
Keep lights above five hundred lumens.
A pause.
They navigate by shadow.
The message repeated.
Keep lights above five hundred lumens. They navigate by shadow.
I checked the equipment. The radio was drawing power from the battery despite the cutoff switch being engaged.
I disconnected it physically.
The message continued for three more repetitions.
Then the speaker emitted a long, low tone, and the room went silent.
I spent the rest of the night testing the claim.
The observatory had an old searchlight mounted on the upper platform. It had been installed decades earlier to illuminate the runway of a now-abandoned mountain airstrip. The lamp still worked, although its reflector was corroded.
I wired it to the generator and pointed it toward the fog.
A broad white beam appeared.
For the first time in seven days, I could see the ground outside the observatory clearly.
The fog retreated from the beam's center.
Not much. A few meters, perhaps.
But it retreated.
At the edge of the light, the darkness gathered into shapes.
Tall shapes.
I counted seven.
Then twelve.
Then too many to count.
They stood where the light ended, their bodies stretched thin by the glare. None had discernible faces. Each had a darker patch where a face should have been.
I increased the light's intensity.
The figures withdrew.
I almost laughed.
For the first time, I had found something the fog didn't want me to do.
I wrote a new rule.
Rule Three... Maintain high-intensity illumination whenever possible. Never allow your shadow to fall across an open doorway.
I didn't know yet that the message had been carefully constructed to make me burn through my remaining fuel.
I only knew that, for the first time, I had a weapon.
And weapons make people reckless.
Days Eight and Nine... The Cost of Light.
I ran the searchlight for four hours each night.
During the day, I used smaller lamps to illuminate the corridors and kept the shutters closed. I arranged the lights so that no part of the building was left in complete darkness.
The effect was immediate.
The footsteps stopped.
The knocks stopped.
The voices became less frequent.
On the ninth day, I saw the first person moving through the valley.
A man in a white shirt, walking slowly along the mountain road.
He had no shoes.
He carried a child's red balloon.
I watched him through the telescope, with the protective filter attached and the eyepiece positioned behind a black cloth. I never looked at the valley with my naked eyes.
The man stopped beneath a streetlight.
He looked up at the observatory.
Then he raised the balloon and let it go.
It floated upward.
Not with the wind, but directly toward me.
The balloon rose past the searchlight beam, past the roof, past the antenna.
It stopped against the eastern window.
Something inside it scratched at the glass.
I backed away.
The scratching became a soft, irregular tapping.
Then the balloon burst.
A tiny black cloud spread across the outside of the window, coating the glass with soot.
I watched it through the narrow gap in the tarp.
The soot slowly arranged itself into letters.
THANK YOU FOR KEEPING IT BRIGHT.
I shut the shutter and spent the rest of the day scrubbing the glass from inside.
By evening, the generator had consumed more fuel than I had planned.
I calculated the remaining supply.
If I continued running the searchlight, I would have nine days of power.
Nine.
I wrote the number on the chalkboard.
Then I crossed it out.
I wrote it again.
I couldn't bring myself to switch off the light.
Not after seeing what waited at its edge.
Day Ten... The Second Voice.
The radio began transmitting at six fourteen in the morning.
No static. No interference.
A man spoke with the tired calm of someone delivering bad news.
To the occupant of the mountain observatory. This is Elias Voss.
I stood so quickly that my chair fell backward.
The logbook lay open beside the receiver.
Do not follow the instructions you received regarding illumination.
I stared at the speaker.
High-intensity light does not repel them, Voss continued. It identifies the location of the living.
A pause.
It makes them gather.
The radio crackled.
Your searchlight has been visible from the valley since the first night. You have shown them exactly where to find you.
My throat tightened.
Who are you? I whispered.
Someone who made the same mistake.
I picked up the microphone.
Where are you?
Below.

The building.

Listen carefully. The fog is not a creature. It is a condition. It changes what the world means to anything inside it. The figures are not what you should fear.

Day Eleven... Rescue.
At eight0three in the morning, a rescue vehicle appeared on the mountain road.
I saw it through the telescope: a white emergency-response truck with blue markings, its headlights shining through the fog. It climbed the last bend and stopped in front of the observatory.
Two figures stepped out.
Both wore emergency responder uniforms. One carried a medical bag. The other held a rectangular sign.
The sign read:
RESCUE TEAM. WE HAVE BEEN LOOKING FOR YOU.
I felt an almost unbearable rush of relief.
I had imagined rescue hundreds of times. In most versions, I ran down the stairs and threw the door open before the vehicle had fully stopped. Sometimes I cried. Sometimes I couldn't speak. Sometimes I woke from the dream just as somebody touched my shoulder.
Now rescue was standing outside my actual door.
I reached for the radio.
Then I remembered Voss.
The part of you that wants to let them in.
I did not respond.
The figure holding the sign raised it higher.
A second sign appeared beneath the first.
WE KNOW YOU ARE ALIVE.
Then a third.
WE CAN SEE YOUR LIGHTS.

The first responder was looking directly at the entrance.
The second was looking at the searchlight.
Neither moved.
After twenty minutes, the first responder walked toward the building.
He climbed the steps and pressed his face against the glass panel beside the front door.
I could not see his eyes.
His head was tilted at an angle that made his neck look broken.
Then he smiled.
He raised his hand and tapped the glass.
Three times.
Exactly as the man on the first day had knocked.
I backed away from the mirror.
A few seconds later, the figure spoke.

Please. It is cold.

I had not heard it in years.
Not since the last time we spoke.

Open the door, son.
My knees buckled.
The figure continued.
I know you are frightened.
I couldn't breathe.
You don't have to do everything alone.

I am proud of you.
My father had never said that.

I crawled into the equipment room and stayed there until the footsteps receded.
When I returned, the truck was gone.
The fog had left two parallel tire tracks on the gravel.
They led toward the building.
They did not lead away.
Day Twelve... The Reflection Test.
I needed a way to distinguish the things outside from ordinary people.
I couldn't rely on voices, uniforms, or behavior. Whatever they were, they could reproduce all three.
I settled on a simple experiment.
Shadows.
The searchlight remained active, illuminating the front steps. I placed a metal tray beside the entrance so that I could see the figures reflected in its polished surface without looking directly at them.
At sixteen twenty-one, somebody knocked.
I checked the tray.
A woman stood on the steps wearing a gray coat. Her face was obscured by a surgical mask. She held a paper cup in both hands.
She raised the cup toward the window.
Steam curled from its opening.
The woman was offering me coffee.
I almost laughed at the absurdity of it. Coffee. On the twelfth day of the end of the world.
She pointed at the cup, then at the door.
I examined her reflection.
The searchlight illuminated her body from behind.
There was no shadow.
I shifted the tray slightly.
Still no shadow.
I watched her move her left hand.
In the reflection, the right hand moved.
I watched her tilt her head.
The reflected head tilted in the opposite direction.
It was not a mirror image.
It was something imitating the idea of one.
Then she removed her mask.
The face underneath was mine.
Not similar to mine.
My face.
The same scar near my eyebrow. The same tired eyes. The same small patch of beard I kept forgetting to shave.
The reflection smiled.
My actual face did not.
The woman outside raised the coffee cup to her lips.
The liquid inside was black.
She drank.
Then she opened her mouth wider than a human jaw should allow.
Something fell out.
A tiny white object struck the step and rolled toward the door.
A tooth.
Mine.
I dropped the tray.
The sound of metal hitting concrete echoed through the observatory.
Outside, the woman began laughing.
I didn't stop running until I reached the upper observation room.
That night, I took a kitchen knife and scratched a new rule into the door.
Rule Four... Do not trust reflections. Do not trust familiar faces.
Below it, I wrote something else.
If you see yourself outside, do not assume you are inside.
I stared at the sentence for a long time.
Then I crossed it out.
I didn't know why I had written it.

The knocking moved underground.

Don't answer, I whispered to myself.

Day Fourteen... A New Rule.
The radio was silent until noon.
Then Voss returned.
Have you seen yourself yet?
I stared at the receiver.
Who are you?
That is not important.
Are you in the building?
No.
Are you dead?
A pause.
That is not important either.

I thought of the voice telling me to open the door.
I thought of my father's words.

Copies.

Of people who look at them.

What does that mean?

Orange.
Warm.
Sunlight.
I could hear birds.
I could hear traffic in the valley.
I could hear the distant rumble of a diesel generator.
And somewhere below, a man was speaking into a radio.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
My voice.
The recording from the second day.
Except this time, I knew what came next.
I knew the sentence would repeat.
I knew the radio would continue speaking after I disconnected it.
I knew I would spend the next thirty days trying to survive.
I stepped away from the hatch.
The sunlight disappeared.
The birds stopped.
The darkness returned.
And I understood.
The staircase was not an exit.
It was a memory.
The fog had been showing me the same moment from different angles, offering me a different choice each time. Every voice, every warning, every rule had led me back to the same decision.
Open the door.
Look outside.
Trust the voice.
The observatory was not a shelter.
It was the place where the decision was being made.
I backed away from the hatch.
The door slammed shut.
The locking wheel turned.
I heard footsteps descending the stairs beneath the dome.
Then silence.
I stood in the center of the room, shaking.
I wanted to believe I had resisted.
I wanted to believe I had made the right choice.
But something was wrong.
I couldn't remember climbing the stairs.
I couldn't remember arriving at the observatory.
I couldn't remember the last time I had seen an actual sunrise.
I looked at the clock.
Six fourteen.
The second hand moved forward.
For the first time in thirty days, it reached six fifteen.
Then six sixteen.
Then six seventeen.
I watched it for a full minute.
Time was moving again.
I laughed.
It came out as a broken, frightened sound.
I began to cry.
I was still alive.
I had survived.
Then the radio switched on.
A woman's voice spoke through the speaker.
Emergency advisory. All survivors should remain indoors. Avoid unshielded windows. Do not respond to voices originating outside your shelter.
I stopped crying.
I knew those words.
They were the first transmission I had received on the fifth day.
The woman repeated the message.
Then she said, This transmission is automated. If you can hear this message, you are not alone.
I approached the radio.
The voice changed.
It became my voice.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
I unplugged the receiver.
It continued speaking.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
I tore the antenna cable from the wall.
The voice continued.
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
I smashed the radio against the floor.
The speaker broke.
The voice continued.
I stepped backward.
The dome was changing.
The walls were becoming familiar.
The telescope was moving toward the center of the room.
The hatch was disappearing into the floor.
And the black fingerprints on my hand were spreading.
I looked down.
My fingers were no longer my fingers.
They were long and thin, the skin darkening into a texture like burned paper. Black residue flowed beneath my fingernails.
I tried to scream.
Nothing came out.
I looked at the window.
The tarps were gone.
Beyond the glass, the sky was a vast, featureless black.
The city below was filled with people.
Thousands of them.
Standing shoulder-to-shoulder.
Every face turned toward the observatory.
Every face was mine.
I stumbled backward.
One of the figures in the crowd raised its hand.
I raised mine involuntarily.
The figure lowered its hand.
I lowered mine.
Then the crowd began to move.
Not toward the mountain.
Away from it.
Every person turned around and walked into the darkness, leaving the streets empty.
For the first time, I saw the city clearly.
It was not abandoned.
It was not destroyed.
It was intact.
The buildings were undamaged. The roads were empty. The streetlights were working.
And above the city, the sun was rising.
I pressed my face against the glass.
The light was beautiful.
Golden.
Warm.
Real.
I began to laugh again.
Then I noticed something in the sky.
The sun was rising in the west.
I stepped away from the window.
A voice spoke behind me.
Beautiful, is it not?
I turned.
A man stood beside the telescope.
He wore my clothes.
He had my face.
He looked exactly as I had looked on the first morning, before the eclipse.
He smiled at me.
You have done very well, he said.
I tried to speak.
The man raised one hand.
Five black fingerprints marked the back of it.
Then he said the words I had heard thirty days earlier, on the radio, before I had understood what they meant.
The fog does not need to enter your building.
He took one step toward me.
It only needs you to believe that it is outside.
I finally understood what had happened.
I had spent thirty days hiding from something that did not exist beyond the walls.
The fog was not surrounding the observatory.
The observatory was inside the fog.
The figures were not trying to enter.
They were trying to leave.
And the man standing in front of me was not an imitation.
He was the person I had been before I started watching.
The person who had looked through the telescope.
The person who had seen the city.
The person who had tried to understand the darkness.
I had been watching him for thirty days.
He had been watching me for forty-three years.
He stepped aside.
Behind him, the telescope pointed toward the eastern horizon.
Its lens cap was off.
I could see the sun through the eyepiece.
I could see the city.
I could see the observatory.
And I could see a man standing at the window, looking into the fog.
A man who had just watched the sun fail to rise.
A man who was about to cover every window with tarps and foil.
A man who was about to write the first rule.
I reached for the telescope.
The man beside me shook his head.
Don't.
I stopped.
Why?
He looked at me with something like pity.
Because if you look, he will see you.
I stared at the eyepiece.
The man in the distant observatory was moving.
He was reaching for a notebook.
He was writing something.
I could read the words.
RULE ONE... NEVER LOOK DIRECTLY INTO THE FOG.
I stepped away.
The man beside me smiled.
Good.
Then he vanished.
The observatory was empty.
The sun was gone.
The city was gone.
The telescope was gone.
I stood alone in absolute darkness, unable to tell whether my eyes were open or closed.
I reached for the wall.
My fingers touched cold metal.
The hatch.
I heard the locking wheel turn.
Once.
Twice.
Then the door opened.
And from somewhere far below, I heard a man whisper my name.
I didn't answer.
I didn't move.
I waited.
The footsteps climbed the stairs.
Slowly.
Patiently.
They stopped directly behind me.
A hand rested on my shoulder.
And a voice, my own voice, whispered into my ear:
You are not going to check?
The following morning, a maintenance crew arrived at the abandoned mountain observatory.
The building had been sealed for forty-three years.
They found the generator empty, the radio smashed, the telescope uncovered, and thirty days of handwritten survival notes scattered across the floor.
There was no sign of forced entry.
No footprints in the dust.
No body.
One technician discovered a notebook beneath the observation dome.
Its final page contained a single sentence, written in black ink.
If you are reading this, do not look through the telescope.
Below it was a date.
October seventeenth, nineteen eighty-three.
The technician frowned.
He checked his watch.
Six thirteen.
Outside, the birds stopped singing.
The technician looked toward the eastern window.
The sky was turning black.
At six fourteen in the morning, the sun failed to rise.
And somewhere inside the sealed observatory, a radio that had been disconnected for forty-three years switched on.
A man's voice whispered...
Hello? Is anyone receiving?
A pause.
Then, in a voice that sounded exactly like the technician's, the radio answered:
Yes.
